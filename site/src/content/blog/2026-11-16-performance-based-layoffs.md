On November 9th, notifications started going out at Qualcomm's offices in Bengaluru, Hyderabad, and Delhi-NCR. The
company was eliminating [over 1,000 roles][trendforce] in India as part of what it called a "performance-focused
reduction." One engineer described on Reddit what that looked like in practice: four years at Qualcomm, performance
ratings of 3, 4, and 4 across three annual reviews, an expectation of a promotion to Senior Engineer, and then a
dismissal notice. Others said they were told explicitly that business requirements, not individual performance, had
driven the decision.

The company's public framing and what affected employees reported do not line up. That's what we're here to discuss.

*Where the label came from*

The template was set at Meta on January 14, 2025. Zuckerberg sent an internal memo announcing that Meta would cut about
5% of its workforce, targeting its lowest performers. [He wrote,][cnbc-meta] "I've decided to raise the bar on
performance management and move out low performers faster." About 3,600 workers were eventually affected.

The problem was that some of those workers [had received favorable performance ratings.][fortune-meta] Employees who had
been marked "at or above expectations" on their 2024 assessments, which at Meta indicated mid-tier standing rather than
low performance, were included in the cuts anyway. Meta called them low performers. The employees disputed that
characterization in public.

[Microsoft followed in January 2025][cnbc-msft] with its own round of performance-based cuts across departments
including security, sales, and gaming, citing the same high-performance standard rationale.

Both companies had something to gain from the framing. "Performance-based" tells investors a different story than "cost
reduction." It signals workforce discipline, a commitment to excellence, the kind of management rigor that belongs in an
earnings narrative rather than a difficult quarterly press release. It doesn't signal a revenue problem or a strategy
shift. The label spread through 2025 and into 2026 because it worked for the audience that cared most about hearing it.

*What the label actually requires*

A performance termination has prerequisites. You have to have managed performance first. That means documented feedback
delivered to the employee, clear expectations set in advance, and a genuine opportunity to meet them. Without that
groundwork, you have a business decision. Describing it as a performance decision is a category error at best.

This isn't a legal technicality. It's a coherence problem. "This person was let go for performance reasons" only makes
sense if there's a performance record that supports it. Without that record, the person being let go has no way to
understand what they could have done differently, because the answer is nothing. The business changed direction. That's
a different kind of event.

The Qualcomm cases make this plain. Some employees say they never received performance feedback before their dismissal
notices arrived. The engineer who expected a promotion hadn't been counseled, put on a plan, or warned. The notices that
[cited business requirements][calcalis] as the reason were honest. The company's public framing was not.

When a company applies the performance label without having run the performance process, the label isn't describing a
decision. It's managing a narrative.

*What was actually happening*

Qualcomm's Q4 FY2026 results told a clear story. [Handset revenue fell 26% year-over-year.][tradingkey] [Automotive
revenue jumped 89%][grafa], hitting Qualcomm's first $2 billion automotive quarter. Those aren't the numbers of a
company cleaning up underperformance. They're the numbers of a company in the middle of a structural pivot, with product
mix shifting under it.

Engineering teams doing handset work weren't performing badly. They were in the part of the business that was shrinking
while another part scaled rapidly. The decision to reduce headcount on the declining side is a rational response to that
shift. What it isn't is a performance story. The engineer writing handset firmware at a 3/4/4 rating, expecting a
promotion, was doing what they were hired to do. The business changed what it needed, not what it had.

Restructuring is an accurate description of that. It tells employees that the business moved, that certain roles no
longer fit the direction, and that the decision wasn't about individual output. That's a harder conversation to have on
an earnings call, because it invites questions about whether the company saw the shift coming and planned for it. But
it's accurate.

*The damage the label does*

Three distinct things happen when the label doesn't match reality.

The first hits the people who were let go. An engineer who expected a promotion and was instead mislabeled a low
performer has to reconcile two things that don't line up. That reconciliation isn't neutral. It lands on their
self-assessment, their next job search, the next time a manager gives them feedback. Someone laid off in a restructuring
knows why it happened. Someone mislabeled as a low performer has to decide whether to believe the label or fight it, and
either way they carry it longer than they should.

The second hits the people who stayed. A [2025 survey][careerminds-aftershocks] found that 55% of respondents cited low
morale among remaining staff and 53% cited loss of trust in leadership as major post-layoff concerns. Those numbers
assume honest framing. When the remaining employees watched a colleague with a solid rating and a pending promotion get
labeled a low performer, they now have direct evidence that the company will misrepresent the reason for a cut. That
evidence doesn't expire. It quietly informs every all-hands, every performance cycle, every assurance from leadership
that things are fine.

The third is a collective action problem. When "performance-based" becomes industry code for "we needed to cut headcount
and this was the least contentious framing," it loses its signal value everywhere. Companies that actually do manage out
low performers, the ones with the documented feedback history, the fair process, and the paper trail, now compete with
that noise. Nobody believes the label anymore, because it has been used too many times to mean something else. Each
company that reaches for it without the underlying work to support it makes the signal slightly less useful for everyone
else.

*What honest looks like*

There's a fear that "restructuring" sounds like an admission that leadership didn't anticipate the shift. Sometimes
that's true. But that's an accountability the company can absorb. The alternative is to distribute the cost to the
people least able to bear it, by giving them a false narrative about their own work.

Qualcomm is pivoting toward automotive and pulling back on handset capacity. That's a coherent business story. It
doesn't require an individual performance failure to justify headcount reduction. Employees can process a pivot. They've
seen companies change direction, and they understand what that means for headcount. What they struggle to process is
being told their work wasn't good enough when they know, and their review record confirms, that it was.

The other thing worth noting is that honest framing tends to be remembered. When a company is straight with people
during a hard decision, the people who stayed through it hold that differently than a decision that felt dressed up.
Trust that survives a restructuring is worth something. Trust that cracks when good employees get mislabeled is
expensive to rebuild, and often it doesn't fully recover.

The label "performance-based" makes sense when it's true. When it isn't, it's a leadership failure wearing a management
mask. The two are not the same thing, and the people on the receiving end of the label know the difference.
